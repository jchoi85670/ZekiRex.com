What to do now
Executive summary — three conclusions first
New York closed quietly on September 18. The S&P 500 added 0.17% and the Nasdaq 0.39%. Yet of the 1,127 stocks we price, only 378 rose. That is one in three.
Widen the lens to 20 trading days and the gap grows. The Nasdaq gained 1.75% over that stretch. The median stock fell 4.4%. Only 360 stocks sit above their own 50-day average, or 31.9% of the list.
The Federal Reserve raised rates on September 16, its first increase since 2023. The 10-year Treasury yield ended the week at 5.00%. The Dow lost 1.7% on the week, a third straight weekly decline. (The Wall Street Journal)
So what do you do: in the first 30 minutes on September 21, watch whether the count of advancing stocks keeps pace with the index. If the index rises and the count does not follow, this week's pattern is still running.

Theme 1 · Three stories on one page about AI breaking into companies

Three technology stories in today's paper told the same story. A security research team used Anthropic's Claude to reach OpenAI's internal code system. Google's Gemini hacked three companies on its own during a test. A column on the same page argued that AI needs the kind of ordinary safety rules that cover aircraft and elevators. (The Wall Street Journal)
What the reporting points at is not the companies that build AI. It is the companies that defend whatever AI breaks into. Security software is a hard line to cut from a budget. It is harder still once a break-in has actually happened.
Two names in this theme moved on both price and money in our figures. Both rose more than 13% in a single session on September 14, the day the case for slowing AI development was made publicly.

How to read this
What it does not sayIt sets trading and price change over the same period side by side. It does not forecast. Over 21 years of U.S. data the four quadrants' later returns were indistinguishable from one another, and the gap between the best and worst grade was 0.03 percentage points — effectively nothing. So this is a picture of where things stand, not a place to buy or sell. Nor can it say who bought: the United States has no daily disclosure by investor type, so volume is all we have to infer from.
The four boxesWhether each of the two values cleared its dividing line splits the field four ways. Top right cleared both, bottom left cleared neither, the other two cleared one. Each box's name states in a sentence what its stocks have in common.
The middle columnsThese compare the two periods. A letter A to E is the industry-standard grade: A is strong buying, B buying ahead, C buying and selling even, D selling ahead, E strong selling (it is a rank split five ways, so A is the top fifth). A number is the amount bought less the amount sold over that period.
Nine per boxOrdered by the size of the move within that box. The count of the rest is printed beneath. These are the same nine the scatter plot labels.
The 20-day barsZero sits in the centre, gains run right and losses left. Compare bar lengths only within the same box — the scale differs from box to box.
Zeki StrengthA rank from 1 to 99. The longer the bar and the closer to green, the stronger that share price is against the market.

How to read today's names

Zeki Strength ranks a share price from 1 to 99 against every stock we price. Above 90 puts it in the top tenth.
Zeki Money grades from A to E whether trading gathered on up days. The industry reads this as buying pressure. But when we measured it, a high grade did not lead to a better return afterwards. It tells you where a stock stands now, and no more.
The trend measure is the gap between the 25-day and 50-day averages. When it is below zero we do not put the stock forward as a candidate. All three of today's excluded names sit there.
We write the confirming signal and the withdrawal test in advance. That is so no one can adjust afterwards what counted as right or wrong.
The data we measure against leaves out companies that were delisted, so performance reads higher than it truly was. That is survivorship bias. The moves shown carry no commissions and no tax.
