Korea's Blood Donors Fall by a Quarter as a Shot to Regrow Teeth Enters Trials

ZekiRex™ Sunday Edition · Sunday, October 4, 2026
The week beyond the markets — People · Society · Science & Technology · Politics
No newspapers are published on Sunday. This edition is built from Saturday's papers.
THIS WEEK
Korea had more than 410,000 fewer blood donors last year than a decade earlier. Those who remain are giving more often.

In Japan, a shot that makes lost teeth grow back has entered human trials.

President Trump announced an $8.4 billion Korean investment, but Seoul says no such deal was agreed.
This Week, People
One person, or one group, and their story
Two high schoolers who heard a building shake
Maeng Min-gyu and Lee Su-in, second-year students at Chungnam Science High School, will receive the presidential prize in the student division of this year's National Science Exhibition. The National Science Museum announced it on October 2. It is the 72nd edition of the contest.

What the two listened to was sound. Tap a structure and it rings. Analyze that ring and you can spot moments when the strength of the vibration suddenly swells and fades. The effect is called "wobble."

They did not set out to find it. They began by asking whether tiny tremors invisible to the eye could be heard, and came across wobble along the way.

Their tools were simple audio equipment and mathematics. Judges said the work shows a way to flag trouble at a building's joints.

The teachers' presidential prize went to three elementary-school teachers in South Jeolla Province. They built a program that shows on screen the forces at work when a Joseon-era flail weapon is swung. (Korea Economic Daily, Oct. 3)

The idea of checking buildings with an ear and some arithmetic, rather than costly sensors, came out of a high-school classroom.
This Week, Society
The week in one number
1.25 million: the fewest donors on record
Last year 1,251,288 people in Korea gave blood at least once. Statistics Korea released the figure on October 2. It is the lowest since records began in 2005.

In 2015 the count was 1,668,424. A quarter of donors have gone in ten years.

Those who stayed are filling the gap. The average donor now gives 2.27 times a year, up from 1.85.

It is still not enough. Directed donations, where a patient's family must find donors themselves, reached 35,197 in January to September alone. That is more than double last year's full-year total.

Platelets are the tightest. They keep for only about five days, so they cannot be stockpiled. Leukemia patients and stem-cell transplant patients depend on them. (Korea Economic Daily, Oct. 3)

Blood cannot be made in a factory. The missing quarter is being covered by the arms of those still showing up.
This Week, Science & Technology
What will still matter in five years
A shot to regrow lost teeth reaches human trials
Toregem BioPharma, a Kyoto University spin-off, is developing an injection that makes teeth grow back. It ran its first clinical trial in adult men in 2024. Within this year it plans a second trial in children born without some teeth. Its target date is 2030.

Here is how it works. Even after all adult teeth are in, the gums keep tooth buds. A protein called USAG-1 stops them from growing. When researchers gave animals an antibody that blocks this protein, new teeth grew. A ferret with six front teeth grew a seventh.

Others are taking different routes. Tufts University in the U.S. combined human dental stem cells with pig tooth cells and implanted them in a pig's jaw, producing tissue similar to a human tooth. King's College London has grown a tooth structure with roots in the lab.

Today's dentistry fills or implants. Fillings last 20 years at most. Implants have no nerves, so they cannot sense biting force. (Korea Economic Daily, Oct. 3)

If the 2030 target holds, in five years the dentist's office starts to become a place that grows teeth rather than patches them.
This Week, Politics
Only what touches money next week
An $8.4 billion investment, two governments, two stories
President Trump posted on social media on October 2. He said Korea would put $8.4 billion into a U.S. oil-recovery project, about 11.3 trillion won.

Seoul tells it differently. Officials said there is no investment agreed with Washington beyond the three U.S. projects already announced.

The project in question is a carbon-capture venture by Summit Carbon Solutions. It would pipe carbon dioxide from Midwest ethanol plants about 2,900 km to Wyoming. The gas would be injected into oil fields to push out more crude. Washington put it forward to Seoul as a lead candidate in May.

After an accounting-firm review, the government reportedly judged the returns too thin and effectively declined. Industry Minister Kim Jung-kwan said on September 22 that the project fell short on commercial grounds. Some analysts read the U.S. announcement as election-season credit-taking. (Korea Economic Daily, Oct. 3)

The link to money next week is one point. The further U.S. announcements run ahead, the more Seoul has to explain in its U.S. investment talks.
In one line: This was a week about how to fill what is missing — fewer donors, lost teeth, unseen cracks in buildings, and an investment nobody signed.
Last week's market, and the week ahead
In the first week after the Chuseok holiday, the KOSPI fell for three days and rose for two. It dropped to 6,838.04 on September 30 and finished at 7,003.74 on October 2. That is still below the pre-holiday close of 7,080.92 on September 23.

Foreigners did the selling. They were net sellers of 21.52 trillion won of main-board stocks in September. They were net sellers of 73.1% of listed names, the highest monthly share this year.

The KOSDAQ closed at 893.29 on October 2. Intraday it topped 900 for the first time since early July. Chip testing, packaging and other materials and parts makers led the index. (Korea Economic Daily, Oct. 3)

In New York on October 2, the S&P 500 closed at 7,723 and the Nasdaq Composite at 27,191. U.S. nonfarm payrolls released that day rose by just 29,000 in September, a third of the 89,000 expected. The 10-year Treasury yield hit 5.34% intraday on October 1, its highest since 2002. (Korea Economic Daily and Maeil Business Newspaper, Oct. 3)
