What You Have To Know Today
The whole yield curve was repriced in one session
On September 10 in New York, WTI rose 6.69% to $102.5 a barrel and Brent 6.34% to $107.6. Yields rose across the whole curve rather than at one end: the 2-year to 4.38%, the 10-year to 4.94%, the 30-year to 5.36%. The short end moving most is the point. The 2-year carries expectations about policy, so the market has shifted from pricing a cut to pricing a hike. Copper fell 4.95% the same day and gold 1.17%. Had this been a yield rise driven by a stronger economy, copper would not have fallen with it.
Why. Two presses read the same barrel differently. US outlets write it as a domestic policy question: oil pushes yields, and yields change the Federal Reserve's next choice. Korean outlets move the same event onto the currency and the cost of funding, treating it as a shock from outside. Our own numbers support the first reading, because the 2-year rose most on the curve while copper and gold both fell. This was not better demand; it was a more expensive dollar of money.
What follows. This reading has already been tested once. In reporting filed between 03:10 and 03:45 PT on September 11, WTI gave back the previous day's gain — and the Treasury selling did not retreat with it. Bloomberg reported the selloff intensifying over the same hours. Had fuel been the cause, yields should fall once the cause is gone. So there are three things to check next edition: whether the 2-year yield stays above 4.38%, whether refining holds first place in the industry ranking, and whether semiconductor equipment holds second.
For what we own: The account fell 3.75% on September 10 against the S&P 500's 0.58% — more than six times as much. The cause is concentration rather than diversification: all four holdings sit on one fork in the road, computing capital spending. Dell fell 5.35%, Micron 4.90% and ASML 2.43%, while the refiner, Marathon Petroleum, gave up only 1.8%. The other side of the case belongs here too: if yields retreat, the same structure returns at the same multiple, because what repriced these names was the discount rate and not their earnings. So for a reader holding semiconductors or computing equipment, the number to watch is not the earnings outlook but the 2-year Treasury yield. Look at where it goes before changing the size of anything.
