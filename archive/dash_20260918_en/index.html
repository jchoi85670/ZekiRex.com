WHAT YOU NEED TO KNOW TODAY
1. Crude fell for a third session on Sept. 17 and West Texas Intermediate slipped below $100 a barrel. What cut the price was a rerouted supply line rather than weaker demand: with its East-West pipeline down, Saudi Arabia is selling forward barrels that avoid the Strait of Hormuz. Until the pipeline is repaired this relief is the reversible kind. (The Wall Street Journal, CNBC, Bloomberg)
2. The 10-year Treasury yield eased to 4.95% from 5.01% the same session, while the 2-year held near 4.40%, narrowing the gap between them to 0.55 percentage points. A cheaper barrel releases the inflation leg of a yield first, and that leg sits in the long end of the curve.
3. The S&P 500 rose 1.14% to 7,638 on Sept. 17 and the Nasdaq Composite 1.69% to 26,418. It was the biggest day in six weeks and both indexes reclaimed their 50-day averages, led by a chip rebound in Intel and AMD. (The Wall Street Journal, Investor's Business Daily)
4. Nvidia's chief executive said on Sept. 17 that the company would sell about twice as many chips next year as this year. The same day King Charles III opened an AI summit with a warning that controls are needed before it is too late. A supplier's forecast and a host's warning landed on one day, and the price moved on the first. (CNBC, Fortune)
5. The Bank of Japan raised its policy rate to 1.25% from 1.0% on Sept. 18 local time, the highest in 31 years. The dollar nonetheless rose to 156.90 yen from 156.14, because Governor Kazuo Ueda's press conference read softer than expected. It means money borrowed in yen and parked in other markets has not come home yet. (The Guardian, Bloomberg)

West Texas Intermediate fell for a third session on Sept. 17 and slipped below $100 a barrel. The 10-year Treasury yield eased the same session while the 2-year held near 4.40%. The S&P 500 rose 1.14% to 7,638 and the Nasdaq Composite 1.69% to 26,418, and both reclaimed their 50-day averages. The Russell 2000 rose 0.55% and stayed below that line. The order matters: crude fell, then yields fell, and then the side that sells equipment rose first. (Reuters, CNBC)
Why. The Korean and American presses count the same session differently. Kyunghyang and Maeil Business named the simultaneous fall in crude and in US yields as the reason for the rebound. The Wall Street Journal led instead with the Bank of Japan's increase making US assets less alluring for Tokyo investors. The gap showed in the currency rather than in equities: after Governor Kazuo Ueda's press conference read softer than expected, the dollar rose to 156.90 yen from 156.14. In Seoul, the Kospi closed 2.66% higher at 6,894.23 on Sept. 18 local time. (Maeil Business, Bloomberg, Yonhap)
What follows. Two things carry into the next edition: the repair schedule for the East-West pipeline and what the Bank of Japan says next. When the pipeline returns, the barrels sold forward disappear; when Japan's next increase becomes certain, the money placed in Asia moves first.
For what we own: If you hold refiners, the line to check is the refining margin rather than the oil headline. Refining profit comes from the gap between crude and product, not from the crude price, and Sept. 17 showed that gap: on a session when crude fell, Phillips 66 (PSX) rose 3.62%, HF Sinclair (DINO) 2.33% and Marathon Petroleum (MPC) 1.94%. The other direction deserves the same space. This relief comes from barrels sold forward while a pipeline is down; those barrels are pulled from the future, so they disappear when the pipeline is repaired. If you hold chips, look somewhere else. The Sept. 17 rebound arrived on a session when yields fell once, and it can retrace at the same place if yields go back. That two of our four positions are refiners is the weakness of this reading: when the margin narrows, both fall together.
