Adult children drop their parents; a bug checker remakes mathematics

This Week, People
One person, or one group
Adult children who stop calling home
Saturday's Wall Street Journal carried a family story. More adult children are cutting off their parents. Many experts believe these estrangements are spreading fast. (WSJ, Oct. 3)

Two causes are named. One is a change in what family members believe they owe each other. The other is a change in how therapy is practiced.

There is a number to size it. In a 2020 survey by Cornell's Karl Pillemer, 27% of American adults said they had cut off a relative.

The same paper carried the opposite story. British writer Sarah Perry wrote a book about caring for her father-in-law after his cancer diagnosis. It is about how hard it is to become the one who stays.

The rule for how close a family stays is moving from duty to comfort. Yet stories of those who stay to the end are still being written.
This Week, Society
One number for the week
$2 million: what local police can earn per immigration arrest
The Department of Homeland Security is paying local police. The money goes to agencies in a nationwide immigration task force. It can reach $2 million per arrest. (WSJ exclusive, Oct. 3)

The point of the report: some agencies collected large sums for only a handful of arrests.

For local police that is real money. The same paper reported on Seattle's mayor. A string of violence and a budget deficit have stalled her agenda. City safety budgets are tight.

Money changes priorities. The open question is which comes first: neighborhood safety or federal arrest counts.
This Week, Science & Technology
What will still matter in five years
A bug finder becomes mathematics' inspector
There is a program called Lean. It was first built to find bugs in Microsoft products. A new book argues it has transformed mathematics, and the Journal reviewed it. (WSJ, Oct. 3)

Lean has a computer check a proof line by line. If a human proof has a gap, it stops right there. Leonardo de Moura began building it at Microsoft Research in 2013.

Mathematicians now use Lean to re-write known theorems one by one. British mathematician Kevin Buzzard leads a project to put Fermat's Last Theorem into it. Google DeepMind's math system AlphaProof also wrote its proofs in Lean.

What lasts is a change in how we trust. Mathematics is learning to trust a proof a machine has checked, not a person's explanation.
This Week, Politics
Only what touches next week's money
Pre-election cash, and the bond market that pays for it
President Trump announced another payment to voters ahead of the midterms. The amount and timing are not yet confirmed. (WSJ)

Where that money lands is the bond market. On Oct. 1 the 10-year Treasury yield touched its highest level since 2002 intraday. It rose about 0.9 percentage point in the third quarter alone, the biggest quarterly jump since 1994. (Maeil Business Newspaper, Oct. 3)

Oil is pushing rates up, and heavy government debt is adding to the risk. (WSJ)

Campaign money moved too. A big group backing Senate Republicans halted spending in the North Carolina Senate race, a seat Democrats hope to take. The money will prop up a candidate in deep-red Kansas.

Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton was expected to be named to lead AI policy across the administration. (WSJ)

The closer the election, the more spending promises. Long-term rates pay first.
In one line: This was a week about what to trust. Families follow comfort over duty, mathematics has begun to trust a machine's check, and markets are pricing pre-election promises through rates.
