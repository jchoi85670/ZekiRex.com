Buffett hands over his chair, and the factory hands work to robots

THIS WEEK
Warren Buffett, 96, stepped down as chairman of Berkshire Hathaway. His son took the chair.

Korea's prosecution service closes at the end of this month, and the police still do not know how many cases they will inherit.

Toyota will put 400,000 robots into its plants from 2028.
This Week, People
One person, or one group of them
The chair changed hands after 56 years
Warren Buffett has stepped down as chairman of Berkshire Hathaway. He is 96.

The company said on September 17 that he had been named chairman emeritus. He stays on the board. The new chairman is his son, Howard Buffett, who has been a director of the company since 1993. (Korea Economic Daily, September 19)

He held the chair for 56 years. He had already left the chief executive's job on January 1 of this year. In a letter released the same day, Buffett wrote that “Father Time has been generous to me.”

Whether a company one man built can run without him is the test that starts now.
This Week, Society
One number that holds the week
160,000 cases, and nobody has said where they go
Korea's prosecution service is abolished at the end of this month. Cases still open on that day pass, as a rule, to the police and to the new Serious Crimes Investigation Agency.

More than 160,000 unsolved cases sit with prosecutors. The police have not been told how many of them they will receive. Last week the National Police Agency asked the Supreme Prosecutors' Office for the figure. (Korea Economic Daily, September 19)

The rule for dividing cases between the police and the new agency has not been settled either.

The police assign investigators before the Chuseok holiday. They need to know where the cases fall in order to place the people.

Heavier than the number itself is that the side receiving it still does not know it. Two weeks remain.
This Week, Science and Technology
What will still matter in five years
A veteran's hands are becoming a company asset
Toyota will bring 400,000 robots of its own design into its plants from 2028. 150,000 go to the parent company and 250,000 to affiliates.

The robot is called Elly and weighs 50 kilograms. It rolls on wheels rather than walking on two legs. Giving up walking kept the design simple enough to deploy in volume. At a European briefing it folded a T-shirt, after practising the motion 1,500 times over two weeks. (Korea Economic Daily, September 19)

The way it learns is the more striking part. A skilled worker wears a device shaped like the robot's fingers and repeats the job as usual, and the robot learns from the motion. Skilled workers at 60 plants worldwide, 18,000 of them, teach it.

Hyundai Motor will put Boston Dynamics' Atlas into its Georgia plant in the same year. Its job is sequencing: picking parts and passing them to the assembly line in the right order for each model. It is the step that takes the most human hands today.

What lasts five years is not the robot count. It is that a motion learned over thirty years becomes something a company can copy.
This Week, Politics
Only what reaches money next week
Buying a company now means buying half of it
The National Assembly's National Policy Committee passed an amendment to the Capital Markets Act on September 17. It carries a mandatory tender offer rule. The two main parties agreed to proceed, so passage within the year is likely. (Korea Economic Daily, September 19)

The rule works like this. Anyone becoming the largest shareholder of a listed company with 25 percent or more must tender for half the shares outstanding plus one. Until now, buying around 30 percent was enough. A largest shareholder already above 25 percent faces the same duty when adding to the stake.

The intent is to protect minority shareholders. Private equity firms raise the opposite worry. If the price of control rises far enough that deals stop happening, minority shareholders lose the exit those deals provided.

Some expect the rule to favour large foreign funds with deeper pockets. It follows last year's commercial code revision, which had already raised the cost of protecting minority holders.
In one line: this was a week of handing things over, all at once — a chairman's seat, a caseload, a craftsman's hands, and ownership of a company. In each, the side receiving is the one that is not ready.
Last week's market, and the week ahead
The KOSPI closed at 6,894.23 on September 18, up 2.66 percent on the day. Across the full week it still fell 0.23 percent: Friday nearly erased what the earlier sessions had taken. The KOSDAQ ended at 827.12, up 0.79 percent on the week.

Two central banks made Friday's rebound. The United States raised its policy rate to 3.75–4.0 percent on September 16, its first tightening in three years and two months. The Bank of Japan lifted its rate from 1.0 percent to 1.25 percent, the highest in 31 years.

What mattered was that no dissent inside the Bank of Japan called for a larger step. That eased the fear of a sudden unwinding of money borrowed cheaply in yen. Samsung Electronics rose 3.73 percent and SK Hynix 6.42 percent, carrying the index. (Korea Economic Daily, September 19)

In New York on September 18, the S&P 500 closed at 7,650.50 and the Nasdaq Composite at 26,522.54. West Texas Intermediate crude stood at $100.30 a barrel.
