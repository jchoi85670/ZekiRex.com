What You Have To Know Today
Supply is cut, and the long rate is heading for 5%
Drone attacks shut Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline over the weekend, and fresh strikes landed around the Strait of Hormuz. Crude passed $108 a barrel, alongside an estimate that roughly 4% of world supply could be disrupted. What matters is where the news arrived. In New York on September 11 the 10-year Treasury yield was already 4.97% and the 30-year 5.35%, and core consumer prices released that day came in above forecasts. A supply shock landed on a market that had just been given one more reason to expect higher prices.
Why. Two presses count the same barrel differently. Western outlets weigh the damage and the barrel count. Korean outlets move the same event onto input costs and flows. Our own numbers point at a third place. Of the 61 names that cleared our bar, 13 are oil-linked, and that is the thickest single block in the list. Refining held first among industry groups, its four names averaging a ZekiRex strength of 95. That strength was there before the price of crude moved. This news did not create a trend; it supplied a reason for one that already existed.
What follows. The place an answer arrives this week is the Federal Reserve meeting. The question is whether a price rise that began in supply can be treated with the policy rate at all. Raising rates reduces demand; it does not reopen a blocked pipeline. So what to watch is not the decision itself but whether the 10-year settles above 5%. The Bank of Japan moves the same week. A policy rate of 1.25% is already in the price, which leaves the outlook published after the meeting as the part still to be learned.
For what we own: If you hold refiners or shipping, the number to watch this week is the refining margin rather than the crude price. Diesel is at a record not because crude is scarce but because the plant that turns it into fuel is. That capacity takes years to build. The other side belongs here too. With the 10-year approaching 5%, businesses priced on future profit are marked down first, and a supply shock that lifts prices while destroying demand does not hold those prices for long. Our own portfolio sits on both sides of that: two refiners at 32.04% and one computer-hardware name at 26.12%. It is a structure in which one side being right makes the other wrong, and we hold it knowing that.

→ The successor's leaning reaches demand for long-dated US paper indirectly, through the shape of the European curve. None of that is in today's price, but it helps decide next year's cost of funding.
Exxon lifts its 2030 LNG sales forecast · Bloomberg reported Exxon Mobil raising its 2030 LNG sales forecast on stronger demand growth, and CNBC carried the Baker Hughes chief executive saying that energy projects are not slowing despite higher costs. (Bloomberg · CNBC)
→ The demand both companies describe is rooted in data-centre power. Those supply contracts are signed over several years, so this reads as a guide to capital plans for the years ahead rather than to today's price.
The $5,000 payment plan needs Congress · Axios carried Speaker Johnson saying the $5,000 payment floated by the president would require congressional approval, and the Washington Post argued the sum would not fix household finances. (Axios · Washington Post Business)
→ If approved, the money goes straight to households, so it reaches prices first and the long-dated issuance schedule second. Today it is still a proposal, and none of it is in the price.
Alberta weighs a Crown corporation for gas pipelines · The Globe and Mail reported that Alberta is weighing a Crown corporation to push natural gas pipeline development, aimed at demand from data centres. (Globe and Mail)
→ More gas transport capacity in North America lowers the ceiling on power prices. That is a multi-year volume condition for the four midstream names on our list.
Korea and the US: The shock reaches New York twice. It arrives first as the price of crude and refined product. It arrives again through the manufacturing costs of Korean and Taiwanese plants, inside the memory and components American buyers purchase. Korea imports all of its crude, so it receives the oil price as a cost rather than as a refining margin. The won stood at 1,345.10 on September 11, and a firmer won takes part of that cost burden back off.
