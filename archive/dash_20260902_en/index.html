What You Have To Know Today
The rate that oil created finished at a 20-month high
The United States and Iran traded strikes, Brent rose 4.6% to $94.70 on 9/1, and the consequence showed up in government bonds before it showed up in equities. The US 10-year rose 4bp to 4.80%, a 20-month high, and the 30-year sits at 5.27%. On the same day the S&P 500 fell 0.71%, the Nasdaq 1.03% and the Russell 2000 1.23%. The order of those declines is the order of sensitivity to rates.
Why. What divides the coverage is whether oil alone is the cause. The Financial Times and the Wall Street Journal put their weight on energy prices. Money Today and Kyunghyang name inflation, government debt and the corporate bond issuance funding AI investment together. If oil were the only cause, rates would fall as crude falls; where debt and supply are mixed in, the rate stays even after crude comes down.
What follows. The week's axis is the US August employment report on 9/4 at 08:30 ET. It is the first look at the labor market since the oil shock, and a strong print hardens the case for a hike and lifts long rates once more. The ECB decides on 9/10 and the Federal Open Market Committee meets 9/15 and 16.
For what we own: While long rates sit at a 20-month high, what gets marked down first is whatever earns its profit furthest out. If you own semiconductors or software, this is a phase where the discount rate rather than the earnings is cutting the price, so the direction of the 10-year is worth checking before reading a coming result as a recovery. If instead you own refining or energy, today's advantage comes from a present profit — the refining margin — and it unwinds at the same speed once crude settles. The one slot filled with a refiner here was not a view that the advantage lasts; the rules pointed to that order.
